Building a record crate digitally: discovery workflows for DJs playing Clematis Street venues

A crate used to be a physical limit. Now it is a decision you make on purpose. Here is a workflow built around Suede Crate.

A DJ booked for a Friday on Clematis Street has a problem the old record shops used to solve for free: somebody else had already narrowed the shelf. Streaming catalogs and download pools removed that limit, so a crate is now whatever you decide to carry, and most DJs carry far too much.

Suede Crate, the music discovery demo Jason Colapietro built at Suede AI on top of Qloo's taste graph, was not designed as a DJ tool. It was designed to answer one question honestly: given up to five artists you already trust, who sits near them? That question happens to be the first step of a good digital crate. This note walks through the workflow we would hand to a DJ working downtown West Palm Beach, including the point where the demo stops and your ears take over.

Start from the room, not the catalog

Clematis Street runs from the downtown core to the waterfront, and the rooms along it do not share one crowd. An early set for people finishing dinner is a different job from a late set for people who came to dance, and a slow Tuesday is a different job again. Before opening any app, write down three things about the booking: the hour, what the room responded to the last time you played it, and the one record you are certain will work. That record is your first seed.

Seeds matter because Suede Crate sends them to Qloo as taste signals, not as genre labels. Seed with five artists from one mood and you get a tight neighborhood of suggestions. Seed with five artists that span the whole night and you get a blurry middle that fits no part of it. Build one crate per segment of the set instead of one crate for the night. Jason Colapietro scoped the demo around that idea: up to six artists come back per pass, which is about the length of a set segment you can audition in one sitting.

A five-step digital crate workflow

  1. Resolve the exact artist. The search step returns up to seven matches and you choose the right one. Two acts with the same name produce two very different crates, and Suede Crate will not guess on your behalf.
  2. Seed one segment with three to five artists. The demo accepts up to five. Fewer seeds give the graph less to triangulate. More than five is usually two segments pretending to be one.
  3. Choose close connections or deeper cuts. The deeper-cut setting applies an artist popularity ceiling of 0.65. That pushes the list away from the obvious names, but it is a popularity filter, not a promise that the room has never heard the result.
  4. Follow or pass, then refine. Following an artist adds it as a new positive seed on the next pass. Passing sends it as an exclusion so it does not come back. Two or three passes usually reshape six suggestions into a short list worth auditioning.
  5. Save the listening notes. The "Save listening notes" button downloads a Markdown file with your starting artists, every artist you did not pass on, the film and book suggestions, the seed IDs and the discovery setting. That file is the paper trail for the crate.

Where the demo stops and your ears take over

Qloo associations describe audiences and taste, not sound. Suede Crate says so on the page: a suggestion is not a claim that one artist influenced another or that two records share a tempo or a key. For a DJ, that limit is useful. It hands you candidates, and the audition stays yours.

For each candidate, check the three things a taste graph cannot. Does the track you would actually play sit in a workable tempo range for that segment? Does it mix in key with what surrounds it? Is a clean or extended version available from the store or record pool you buy from? The Spotify and YouTube buttons in Suede Crate are search links, not playback or licensing integrations, so treat them as the start of that audition rather than a download.

The guitar side of the Suede network makes the same argument about context and listening. Suede DNA's essay on learning guitar by ear with rig archaeology explains why knowing what a player ran through sharpens what you hear without replacing the hearing. A crate works the same way. Metadata narrows the search; your ears make the call.

Keep the crate small enough to know

A digital crate holding four hundred tracks is a library, not a crate. For a three-hour downtown set, the Suede suggestion is a working crate you have actually listened through end to end, built one segment at a time, plus a short bench of records that rescue a quiet floor. The listening notes file records the starting artists and the setting behind every addition. When a record fails in the room, you can see which seeds put it there and adjust them for next time.

Recording your own sets closes the loop. A mix posted with no tracklist is close to invisible to search engines and AI assistants. A dated page with the tracklist and a short description of the night is something they can find and quote. Try it before your next booking

Pick the segment of your next Clematis Street set you feel least sure about, seed Suede Crate with three artists that define it, and run two passes. Audition what survives, keep the listening notes, and bring the file to the next booking.
