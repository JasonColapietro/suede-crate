Metadata that makes music findable in AI answers: what Suede Crate's demo models

An assistant cannot recommend a name it cannot resolve. Suede Crate shows, field by field, what clean music metadata looks like.

Ask an AI assistant who to listen to if you like a particular band and the answer is assembled from entities, not from songs. If an artist, a label or a venue is not a clean, identifiable entity on the open web, there is nothing for the answer to point at, however good the music is.

Suede Crate, the Qloo-powered discovery demo Jason Colapietro built at Suede AI in West Palm Beach, is a small working model of that machinery. Every step in it depends on metadata being exact. This note reads the demo as a lesson for musicians, labels and venues in Palm Beach County who want to be the name an assistant gives back.

Answers start by resolving a name to an entity

Suede Crate never sends a typed artist name straight into a recommendation. It first calls Qloo's search endpoint, restricted to the type urn:entity:artist, and shows up to seven matches. The listener picks one, and from then on the app works only with that entity's ID. Any result whose declared type does not match the requested kind is dropped before it reaches the page.

AI search follows a similar order. Before an assistant can recommend or describe an artist, it has to decide which artist a name refers to. Two bands sharing a name, a solo act that also tours under a project name, or a venue that changed its name last year all make that decision harder. The fix on your side is boring and effective: one spelling of the name everywhere, one canonical page per entity, and links between that page and the profiles you control, so every mention resolves to the same thing.

The five fields Suede Crate keeps

When Qloo returns an entity, Suede Crate keeps only five things: the ID, the name, the type, a short description and up to five tags. Names are trimmed to 180 characters and descriptions to 700. Everything else is discarded. The constraint is deliberate. A field the app cannot verify is a field it would be tempted to embellish.

That list is a useful template for an artist or label site, because it is close to what answer engines extract:

  • A stable identifier. In structured data, an @id you reuse on every page that mentions the artist, instead of a new one per template.
  • One exact name. The same capitalization and punctuation on the site, the streaming profiles and the venue listings.
  • A precise type. A band is a MusicGroup, a release is a MusicAlbum or MusicRecording, a show is a MusicEvent. Mixing them up is the schema version of a type mismatch.
  • A short description that stands alone. One or two sentences naming the city, the sound in plain words and one fact a stranger can check, such as a release date.
  • A few honest tags. Three to five genres or scenes you would defend in conversation, not twenty keywords.

What the demo refuses to model

The more instructive half of Suede Crate is what it leaves out. It does not ask a language model to explain why two artists belong together, because a model would produce a plausible reason with no source. It does not invent song titles, influences or match scores. Its Spotify, YouTube, IMDb and Google Books buttons are plain search links, and the project documentation states they are not verified playback, playlist or licensing integrations.

Answer engines reward the same restraint indirectly. They repeat what is publicly documented and consistent across sources. A press bio that claims a "legendary" status nobody else mentions gives an assistant nothing to corroborate. A page that says the band formed in West Palm Beach in a given year, played a named festival on a named date and released a record with a linked tracklist gives it three checkable facts. Suede has seen this from the other side: Jason Colapietro's company keeps a public evidence page with a receipt for each claim, because receipts are what get repeated.

Give assistants a door, not just a page

Suede Crate also publishes an MCP endpoint with two tools, search_artists and build_music_crate. An assistant that supports MCP can resolve an artist and build a crate by calling those tools directly, against the same real Qloo data the web page uses. Nothing has to be scraped or guessed.

At Suede we tell most artists and venues they do not need an MCP server. The cheaper version of the same idea is an llms.txt file and a clean sitemap: a short, plain-text summary of who you are, where you are and which pages hold the facts, linked from the root of the site. This site ships one, as do other sites in the Suede network. For a band, the equivalent might list the bio page, the discography, the upcoming shows page and the press kit.

A metadata checklist for Palm Beach County musicians and venues

  1. Pick the canonical spelling of your name and fix every profile that differs.
  2. Publish one bio paragraph that names your city, your sound and one dated fact, and reuse it word for word.
  3. Add MusicGroup or MusicVenue structured data with a single reusable @id and sameAs links only to profiles you control.
  4. Give every release and every show its own page with a date, a tracklist or lineup, and credits.
  5. Remove any claim you cannot link to a source, then add an llms.txt that points to the pages that remain.
